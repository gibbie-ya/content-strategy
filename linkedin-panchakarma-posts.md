## Post 1 of 5 — Vamana (Therapeutic Emesis)

**The first of the five Panchakarma therapies — and perhaps the most misunderstood.**

Panchakarma translates from Sanskrit as "five actions." Each action is a distinct cleansing procedure designed to draw accumulated toxins out of the body through a specific pathway. Together, they form one of the most thorough and considered detoxification systems in the world.

The first is Vamana — therapeutic emesis, or medicated vomiting.

It is, understandably, the stage that surprises people most. But in Ayurveda, the lungs, stomach and upper respiratory tract are considered the seat of Kapha dosha — the earth and water element. When Kapha accumulates over time, it can manifest as congestion, heaviness, sluggishness, mucus-related conditions, and a kind of thick, unshifting lethargy that no amount of rest seems to touch.

Vamana works by encouraging the body to release this excess Kapha upward, through the stomach. It is not harsh. It is carefully prepared for — the body is first oleated with ghee internally over several days, and then treated with steam to loosen and mobilise what needs to move. Only then does the emesis take place, under close supervision, using herbal preparations chosen for the individual.

The relief people describe afterwards is often remarkable. A clearing. A lightness they hadn't felt in years.

At our clinic in West Lancashire, we work in close collaboration with our Vaidya, Amitha Rudraraju — an Ayurvedic doctor trained in India. She determines which stages of Panchakarma are right for each person, and in what order. Nothing is applied as a template.

I'll be sharing each of the five stages over the coming weeks for anyone curious about what Panchakarma actually involves.

## Post 2 of 5 — Virechana (Therapeutic Purgation)

**The second Panchakarma stage — and the one we use most often at our clinic.**

Virechana is therapeutic purgation: a carefully managed cleansing of the intestines using herbal laxatives, designed to remove excess Pitta dosha from the body.

Pitta is the fire and water element. It governs digestion, metabolism, the liver and gallbladder, the skin, and in many ways our capacity to process — food, emotions, stress. When Pitta builds up beyond what the body can manage, it tends to show up as inflammation, acid, skin conditions, irritability, or that particular kind of depletion where you feel burned out rather than simply tired.

Virechana targets the small intestine and liver directly, drawing accumulated toxins downward and out through the digestive tract.

At our clinic, Virechana is usually taken at home before the client arrives for the rest of their Panchakarma programme. Our Vaidya Amitha prescribes the specific herbal preparation and dosage individually — the preparation used, and the timing, will differ from person to person depending on their constitution and current state of health.

It requires a day or two of rest and simplicity afterwards. The body has done real work. That deserves to be honoured.

What often follows is a noticeable settling — of digestion, of skin, of mood. People describe feeling cleaner from the inside out.

Virechana is the second of the five Panchakarma stages. Next week I'll be sharing the third — Basti, which Ayurveda considers the most powerful of all five.

## Post 3 of 5 — Basti (Medicated Enema Therapy)

**Ayurveda calls Basti the most powerful of all five Panchakarma therapies. Here's why.**

Basti is medicated enema therapy — the introduction of herbal oils, decoctions, or specially prepared combinations into the colon. In Ayurveda, the colon is considered the primary seat of Vata dosha, the air and space element that governs movement, the nervous system, circulation and elimination.

When Vata becomes aggravated — through stress, irregular routine, travel, overwork, poor sleep, or simply the accumulation of years — it tends to affect everything. The joints stiffen. Sleep becomes difficult. The nervous system feels frayed. Digestion becomes unpredictable. There is often a sense of being unmoored, scattered, or simply worn very thin.

Basti works by reaching the colon directly, nourishing and calming the tissue, and drawing toxins out through the bowel. Because Vata governs all movement in the body, bringing it back into balance can have a profound effect far beyond the digestive tract.

There are two main forms: Anuvasana Basti uses medicated oil and is deeply nourishing; Niruha Basti uses herbal decoctions and is more focused on elimination. Our Vaidya Amitha determines which is appropriate — often a sequence of both is used over several days, each chosen specifically for the individual.

We offer Basti as part of our full Panchakarma programmes here at the clinic. It is also available as a standalone treatment in gentler forms — the Matra Basti, for example, is a small, nourishing oil enema suited to those with spinal, joint or intestinal concerns.

People are often surprised by how gentle and restorative it feels. There is nothing harsh about it. It is careful, unhurried, and deeply settling.

## Post 4 of 5 — Nasya (Nasal Therapy)

**The nose, in Ayurveda, is considered the gateway to the head — and to the mind.**

Nasya is the fourth of the five Panchakarma therapies, and it is the one that tends to surprise people most pleasantly.

The treatment involves the administration of medicated herbal oil through the nostrils, after a preparatory head, neck and shoulder massage and the application of herbalised steam. The steam gently opens the nasal passages and surrounding tissues; the oil is then carefully introduced into each nostril while the client rests.

The purpose is to cleanse and nourish the sinus passages, the head and the sense organs — eyes, ears, nose, throat — and to address any excess Kapha or Vata that has accumulated in this region of the body.

In practical terms, people come to Nasya for all sorts of reasons. Chronic sinus congestion. Recurring headaches. Ear and throat issues. Difficulty with concentration or mental clarity. Disrupted sleep. Hay fever. Stiffness through the neck and shoulders. There is also a more subtle dimension to Nasya — because the head is so intimately connected to the mind, a well-performed Nasya can bring a quiet clarity that is difficult to describe and easy to feel.

At our clinic, Nasya sits within our full Panchakarma programme as one of the three primary procedures we carry out here in the UK. It is delivered by our trained technicians and preceded always by the preparatory massage and steam — the sequence matters.

We also offer it as a standalone treatment for those who are not yet ready for a full programme but feel drawn to what it addresses.

If you've been carrying a heaviness in your head for some time — or a fogginess that just won't shift — it might be worth a conversation.

## Post 5 of 5 — Raktamokshana (Blood Purification)

**The fifth and final stage of Panchakarma — rarely used in the UK, but important to understand.**

Raktamokshana is the Ayurvedic approach to blood purification. The name translates as "liberation of blood," and it is the most specialised of the five Panchakarma therapies — used specifically when blood tissue itself is considered to be at the root of a condition.

In classical Ayurveda, Raktamokshana was performed through controlled venesection or through the use of leeches, and it addressed conditions where toxins had entered the blood — certain skin disorders, deep inflammatory conditions, or situations where the other four therapies were insufficient on their own.

It is rarely practised in the UK. At our clinic we do not offer it, and it is important to say that clearly rather than gloss over it. A responsible Panchakarma practitioner will always be transparent about what they do and do not offer, and why.

What we do offer — and what will address the needs of the vast majority of people who come to us — are the three primary procedures most suited to life and bodies in the UK: Virechana, Basti and Nasya. These are chosen and sequenced by our Vaidya Amitha Rudraraju specifically for each person, following a full Ayurvedic consultation including traditional pulse diagnosis.

Panchakarma is not a menu. It is a system. And within that system, what matters is not completing all five stages, but completing the right stages for the right person at the right time.
