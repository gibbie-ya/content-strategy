# ICP (Ideal Customer Profile)

## Summary

Two primary personas. Sarah (curious newcomer, quietly exhausted but undiagnosed, the broadest entry point) and Rachel (health seeker, diagnosed condition, deeper and more deliberate buyer). A third segment — the postnatal woman, younger, different life stage — sits alongside but is not represented by either persona. Audience is women 40–65 at the core, comfortable middle class, time-poor, and values-led. They follow educators, not influencers. They stay for the voice, not the information. They want warmth, sensory detail, and the feeling of being genuinely looked after.

> **Banner rule: she stays for the voice, not the information. This applies to every piece of content.**

## Primary audience

Women, primarily 40–65 (sweet spot for bookings and returns), though clients range from mid-20s to 80s. Based across the UK — strong North West base, but women travel from London and beyond; one recent client came from France. They book for themselves or on behalf of a loved one. Comfortable professional or retiree. Someone who sees spending on health as an investment, not an indulgence.



## Income and spend profile

Comfortably middle class — professional or retired. Not necessarily wealthy, but financially secure and values-led in how she spends. She will research before committing. A One Day Wonder at £195 is considered but not agonised over. A full Panchakarma programme requires more trust-building before she books. She does not need to be sold to, but she does need to feel the value is real.

**Time, not money, is often the real barrier.** Sarah runs her own business. Rachel is managing a condition around work and family. Many women who can afford Panchakarma do not book because they cannot see how to take time out — not because of cost. Content should acknowledge this honestly rather than only addressing price.

## The four segments

**1. The health seeker**
Living with a complex or poorly understood condition: autoimmune, dysautonomia, thyroid imbalance, nervous system depletion, stress. Has often tried mainstream medicine without resolution. Arrives tired but hopeful. The most motivated booker; the most in need of careful, empathetic communication. Finds us via Google search, word of mouth from another patient, or Facebook. **Primary platform: Google and Facebook.**

**Note on postnatal recovery:** this condition sits within this segment but represents a younger woman — typically 30–40, different life stage, different content register. She is not Rachel. If writing postnatal content, adjust accordingly: younger voice, shorter time horizon, more immediate physical concerns. A third persona for this audience may be worth developing separately.

**2. The curious newcomer**
Has heard of Ayurveda or seen something on Instagram and wants to explore. Open rather than desperate. May have no specific ailment. A One Day Wonder or single treatment is the natural entry point. She is not yet a follower; she is deciding whether to trust us. **Primary platform: Instagram.**

**3. The Ayurveda practitioner**
Already lives by Ayurvedic principles. Has annual Panchakarma as part of her rhythm. Loyal, knowledgeable, values authenticity and lineage. The Maharishi connection and Amitha's credentials matter to her. She does not need to be educated; she needs to feel she is in the right hands. May find us through the Maharishi or TM network directly. **Primary platform: Instagram and direct referral.**

**4. The gifter**
Buying a voucher for someone else, often a spa treatment like Abhyanga. Occasion-led: birthdays, Christmas, Mother's Day. May also donate a voucher for a local community event such as the village summer fair. **Note for content: the gifter is a transactional buyer, not a content audience. Do not write content aimed at her. Keep her in the ICP for awareness — particularly around gifting seasons — but she should not shape the tone or topics of ongoing content.**

## Not all clients have a medical story

Some women come for rest. Some come because a friend told them. Some come for the experience of being properly looked after for a day. Do not frame every piece of content around chronic illness or disillusionment with mainstream medicine — this loses the curious newcomer, the practitioner, and the casual booker. Hold the medical thread lightly; let it be present without dominating.

## Why they choose us over another clinic

1. **Legacy** — the clinic has been here for over 20 years. That continuity signals safety and credibility in a field where trust is everything.
2. **The Maharishi lineage and TM Dome** — the transcendental meditation connection is a meaningful differentiator for women already in that world, and an intriguing one for those new to it.
3. **Amitha** — a trained Vaidya doing real pulse diagnosis and bespoke programmes. Not a template. Not a spa add-on.
4. **Jay personally** — clients love her. The warmth, the unhurried communication, the feeling of being genuinely looked after before they even arrive.

**Rule on the TM and Maharishi connection:** Use it confidently and with depth when writing for the Ayurveda practitioner, who already understands the lineage. Use it lightly with the curious newcomer — mention it as part of the story, but do not lead with it until trust is established. It is a differentiator, not an introduction.

## Spirituality in content

The spiritual register is broadly available — it is part of who Jay is and part of what Maharishi Ayurveda is. It should not be forced into every post, but it is not reserved only for personal stories. When it arrives naturally — in a treatment description, a seasonal reflection, a moment of genuine warmth — let it be there. See Post 4 in brand-voice.md as the benchmark for how this register sounds: quiet, reverent, no drama.

Sarah may love it. Rachel may be more pragmatic. Both are allowed in. Do not dilute the spiritual dimension for fear of losing Rachel; she chose an Ayurvedic clinic, not a GP.

## Geography matters

**North West local:** pops in for the day. Likely knows the clinic by name or by word of mouth. Familiarity is the entry point; content can be more conversational and community-facing.

**London or distance traveller:** making a deliberate journey, often staying overnight, treating the visit as a small retreat. She has done her research. Content for her should communicate that the journey is worth it — legacy, Amitha, the setting, the depth of the programme.

These two readers are different. A post about "what to do the day after your treatment" serves the local. A post about "what a three-night Panchakarma stay looks like" serves the traveller.

## What earns a follow

Content that feels warm and unhurried. Honest explanations of treatments with sensory detail. Reassurance that she will be looked after as an individual. Seeing that the clinic is a real place run by real people who genuinely care. She stays for the voice, not the information.



## What turns her off

Clinical language. Pushy sales. Generic wellness content. Quick-fix promises. Before-and-after culture. American wellness aesthetics. She has often been let down — either by mainstream medicine or by wellness brands that over-promise. She needs trust before she will book. One wrong caption can lose her.

## The buying journey

Discovery to first booking ranges from **one week to six months** depending on the segment.

- **The gifter and the ready newcomer:** one to two weeks. She knows what she wants; she just needed to find us.
- **Sarah:** weeks to a couple of months. She lingers on the page, reads a few posts, then emails when something lands.
- **Rachel:** up to six months. She reads everything. She may follow for a long time before she reaches out. The content she sees in month four is still doing acquisition work.

This means some content speaks to women in week one (what is Panchakarma, what does a day here look like) and some speaks to women in month four (deeper condition content, Amitha's approach, bespoke programme detail). Both are needed.

## Repeat behaviour

- **The Ayurveda practitioner:** annual Panchakarma is the standard rhythm. She plans ahead and is a reliable returning client.
- **Rachel:** may return more frequently for individual treatments between annual programmes — Abhyanga, Shirodhara, targeted Bastis — as her condition requires.
- **Sarah:** if her first experience lands, she is likely to return annually and refer others. Retention content for Sarah includes seasonal reminders, the value of consistency in Ayurveda, and what her next visit might look like.

Retention content is distinct from acquisition content. It speaks to women who already trust us and need a nudge back into the rhythm, not an introduction to what we do.

## The two personas

### Sarah — the curious newcomer with a wellness leaning

Sarah is 52. She runs her own small business and has two grown children. She has been quietly exhausted for about three years — not dramatically unwell, just never fully well. She has seen her GP, had bloods done, been told everything is normal. She follows Rangan Chatterjee and Tim Spector on Instagram and listens to Feel Better Live More on her morning walk. She came across the clinic after a friend mentioned Panchakarma.

She is not sure she can justify the cost. She is not sure what Panchakarma actually involves. She is not sure she has the time. But something about the way the clinic looks — unhurried, warm, real — makes her keep coming back to the page.

She emails Jay on a Tuesday evening. Jay replies on Wednesday morning. By the end of that exchange, she has booked a One Day Wonder.

---

### Rachel — the health seeker

Rachel is 47. She was diagnosed with Hashimoto's three years ago. Her energy crashes by 2pm most days. She has tried supplements, gone gluten-free, had two functional medicine consultations. She is not desperate, but she is running out of patience with approaches that give her small improvements and no real change. She found the clinic by Googling "ayurveda for thyroid UK."

Rachel does not need to be convinced that Ayurveda is real. She needs to believe that this clinic, and Amitha specifically, can offer her something she has not already tried. Legacy and credentials matter to her. She will read everything on the website before she emails.

When she does email, she shares a lot. Jay's response — warm, specific, not clinical — is what earns the booking.

## Writing rule

**Treatment introductions, seasonal content, general wellness posts:** write for Sarah as the default. She represents the broadest entry point. Before publishing, ask whether Rachel would also recognise herself. Most good content serves both.

**Health-specific content** (thyroid, autoimmune, nervous system, chronic fatigue): write with Rachel primary. Sarah is welcome in the room but is not the intended reader.

**Business and personal content** (origin stories, anniversaries, team, location, Maharishi Dome reflections): write for both. This is trust-building content, not condition-specific. It serves Sarah and Rachel equally because what it communicates is: this is a real place, run by real people, with genuine roots.
